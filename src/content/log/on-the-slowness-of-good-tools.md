Every tool I own that I would replace immediately if it broke is slow. The film
camera, obviously. The dip pen. The bench supply with the analogue meters that
I keep meaning to upgrade and never do.

This is a suspicious observation to make about your own possessions, because
the alternative explanation is simply that I am slow and have chosen tools that
flatter me. I want to argue against that, and I am aware that wanting to argue
against it is not evidence.

## The honest version

Here is what I think is actually happening. A slow tool puts a gap between the
intention and the result, and the gap is where the decision lives. Thirty-six
frames means the thirty-seventh thing I see does not get photographed, so the
first thirty-six have to be chosen. The choosing is the work.

A fast tool does not remove the choosing. It relocates it — to afterwards, to
the edit, to the moment where you have four hundred frames and no memory of
what you meant by any of them.

## Where the argument fails

It fails for anyone who is actually good. Watch someone competent with a fast
tool and there is no relocation at all; the choosing happens at full speed,
continuously, invisibly. The gap I need is scaffolding, and scaffolding is what
you use while you are still building.

So the honest form of this essay is not "slow tools are better." It is: I am
not good enough yet to go quickly, and I have arranged my hobbies so that I do
not have to admit it very often. Writing that down is the first time I have.
